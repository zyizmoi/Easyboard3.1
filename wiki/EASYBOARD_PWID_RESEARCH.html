Travel independence for PWID

PWID means people with intellectual disabilities. This is what the research says, what is now in EasyBoard, and what still needs testing with the people it is for.

01 / Summary

The short version

EasyBoard already had photo places, calling, and location sharing. This build turns those pieces into a calm travel coach. It does not use live bus data. It uses clear steps, manual confirmation, and a human fallback.

How to read the status labels. Shipped means the feature is in the app on this branch. Partly shipped means the core is live, with a named gap. Next means it is still a proposal. The research and the mockups keep their original shape so the reasoning is visible.

Promise 01 · Shipped
“I always know the next step.”

One instruction at a time, with a photo when the caregiver has added one, a spoken version on the trip, and one clear action.

Promise 02 · Shipped
“I can get help without explaining everything.”

A help card with the person’s name, destination, location and caregiver’s number. One tap to call. One tap to show a stranger. Emergency 999 is separate.

Promise 03 · Shipped
“I decide what my caregiver can see.”

Three sharing choices, a visible state in the header, and a pause that stops the foreground watch. The native background task cleanup is still a known gap. The caregiver cannot change it from their own screen.

Build first

Five changes that matter most

  1. Guided Trip Mode. Shipped One step per screen, with a boarding confirmation, manual advance, progress, and an “I’m not sure” button. No live bus feed yet.
  2. Read Aloud and Easy Read. Partly shipped Three text sizes, an Easy read setting, and speech on the trip screens. Not yet on every screen. Reading settings are stored per user on the device; the privacy choice also syncs to the account.
  3. Help Card. Shipped A full-screen card the person can show to a driver, staff member, or stranger, with a call button and 999.
  4. Trip Story. Shipped A visual preview of the whole trip, with caregiver photos when they have added them, and a practice start.
  5. Calm change alerts. Partly shipped “The bus did not stop” and “I missed my stop” are in the trip. Live delay, route change, and off-route alerts are not.
Avoid

What not to build

  • Always-on location by default. It turns support into surveillance and can push people to stop using the app.
  • Dense route cards. “Route 1, estimated distance, estimated journey time” is a planning screen, not a travelling screen.
  • Red alarm states for normal problems. A late bus is not an emergency. Calm words and a next action work better.
  • Text-only instructions. Reading is not the only way to understand. Pair text with photos and audio.
  • A caregiver-only product. The person with a disability is the user. The caregiver is a supporter.

02 / Users

Who we design for

PWID are not one group. Reading levels, phone skills, anxiety, support needs, and family rules vary. Design for the edges and the middle gets easier. These personas are composites built from the research, not real customers.

R

Student · mild support needs
What he needs
To get from school to home on bus 2 without a teacher following him.
What gets in the way
He recognises some numbers but not all. He gets anxious when the bus does not come. He waits until the last second to press the bell.
Design clue
Show a photo of the stop and the bus number. Tell him how many stops are left. Give him a practice run before the solo run.
S

Siti, 29

Works in a café · mild support needs
What she needs
To meet friends after work without her mother watching every move.
What gets in the way
She reads short sentences. She does not want to be tracked all day. She has been spoken over by staff who assumed she could not decide.
Design clue
Let her choose what is shared and for how long. Use plain words. Make “pause sharing” as easy as “start sharing”.
M

Mei Leng, 58

Transition coordinator
What she needs
To set up a route once, teach it, and know when a student is stuck.
What gets in the way
She cannot watch a live dot for 30 students. She needs exceptions, not a control room.
Design clue
Alerts for “not moving for 10 minutes”, “off route”, “arrived”, and “asked for help”. A trip card, not a map wall.
T

Mr Tan, 62

Parent and caregiver
What he needs
To know his son is safe without calling him every 10 minutes.
What gets in the way
He worries about traffic, strangers, and the phone battery dying. He does not want to be a spy.
Design clue
Simple status: “On the bus”, “10 minutes away”, “Arrived”. A voice note is faster and warmer than a text.

Design for different support needs. Some people will use the app alone. Some will use it with a support worker sitting beside them. Some will not read at all. The same journey should work with touch, voice, pictures, and a phone call.

03 / Findings

What the research says

Ten findings, each with the evidence behind it and what it changes in the product. The pattern is consistent: the barriers are less about maps and more about uncertainty, memory, communication, control, and the small transitions that apps skip, like boarding.

01

Travel is a chain of small transitions, not one route

A scoping review of 34 studies found barriers at every link of the travel chain: walking to the stop, waiting, boarding, riding, getting off, and walking to the destination. The barriers included long waits, missing information, unclear signage, crowding, and driver attitudes.[2] The transport-tech review makes the same point: most studies test one context, such as a single bus route or pedestrian travel, not a full multi-modal trip.[3]

For EasyBoardThe unit of design is the transition, not the route line. “Walk to the stop. Wait here. Get on bus 2. Get off in 8 stops.” Each one needs its own screen and its own help.
02

The hardest part is uncertainty, not distance

In a UK Department for Transport study, people with learning disabilities and other non-visible impairments said the lack of real-time information, route changes, and delays caused the most stress. Some avoided buses completely. Journey apps helped because they reduced the amount of information people had to hold in their heads.[7]

For EasyBoardWhen something changes, do not show a new route. Show one sentence: “The bus is 6 minutes late. Wait here.” Then one action: “Tell me when it comes.”
03

Asking for help is the hardest step

The same UK study found that communicating with staff or strangers caused anxiety and embarrassment. Participants avoided asking questions and used apps or called family instead. They suggested free assistance cards that tell staff what the person needs without a long explanation.[7] The JAM Card scheme in Ireland now has more than 35,000 cards and app registrations; the card lets a person ask for “just a minute” of patience.[10]

For EasyBoardA help screen is a social tool. It should be readable at arm’s length, work without speech, and say exactly what the person needs.
04

Route knowledge is fragile, and practice builds it

At MINDS, a student needed 16 weeks of travel training to go from school to home alone. He could not recognise all bus numbers and wanted to board every bus with a “2”. The team used placards, repeated the route twice a week, and simulated problems such as missing a stop.[8] The research review found that travel training improved confidence and use of public transport.[2]

For EasyBoardThe app should support rehearsal as well as live navigation. A practice mode with the caregiver, a “what if” quiz, and a “solo ready” checklist fits how travel skills are actually learned.
05

Context-aware prompts work, but the evidence is small

A review of 13 studies found that digital tools improved independent travel. In one small study, 8 of 11 participants using a GPS device completed the bus route; in the control group, 1 of 12 got off at the correct stop. In another, 17 of 18 participants using an augmented-reality app reached and identified the destination, compared with 9 of 18 using Google Maps. A third found 93.3% wayfinding success with a PDA and QR tags; four of six participants failed without the aid.[3] These are promising but small studies, often on one route.

For EasyBoardUse GPS to trigger prompts, but do not depend on it. Let the person tap “I’m here” when GPS drifts. Always keep a caregiver call and a human fallback.
06

Most travel apps are not designed with this group

In the 13-study review, only 2 studies involved PWID in the design of the technology. Most focused on a single context and did not explore communication, buying tickets, crowds, or asking for help.[3] A separate study of ride-hailing apps found that PWID hit their own difficulties, and recommended family and government support.[4]

For EasyBoardCo-design is part of the work. Test with people with different reading levels and support needs. Pay them for their time. Ship the version they can use on a real route.
07

Easy Read is a system, not a font size

NHS England guidance says easy read should use short sentences of about 15 words, one idea per sentence, simple punctuation, active language, and images that match the text. It should be tested with the audience. Images can distract some autistic people, so easy read should be a choice, not a forced mode.[6] The CDC guide for PWID with very low literacy is stricter: aim for 10 words or fewer per sentence, one idea per screen, literal images, and one action per screen.[18] The evidence that easy read improves understanding is limited. A 2026 systematic review of 29 studies found that people valued easy read, but the research quality was low and the effect was unclear.[21] The W3C cognitive accessibility guidance adds consistent layouts, clear headings, familiar controls, and help that is easy to find.[5]

For EasyBoardBuild an Easy Read setting that changes text, spacing, and images across the app. Keep an audio version. Test the actual screens with people with different reading levels; do not assume pictures help everyone.
08

Safety tools can become control tools

Research on surveillance in care settings finds a persistent tension between safety and freedom. Professionals worry about restriction, and the ideal use depends on whether everyone affected understands what the technology is for.[12] PWID describe carer gatekeeping and restriction as harmful to their wellbeing. They want support to manage risk, not removal of the chance to take it.[11]

For EasyBoardMake sharing consensual, visible, and time-limited. Default to “share this trip”, not “share my location forever”. Let the person pause without a fight.
09

Digital exclusion is part of the design brief

PWID remain more digitally excluded than the general population, and support staff sometimes restrict access because of perceived risk.[11][13] In an Icelandic survey of 144 PWID, 25.2% said they never or almost never used phone or digital technology to contact others.[20] A phone can run out of battery, lose signal, or run out of data. Not everyone has the latest device.

For EasyBoardCache the route. Make the help card work offline. Provide an SMS fallback and a printable card. The safety plan cannot depend on a live connection.
10

Boarding is its own step

Travel-training assessments list “knows the bus number”, “identifies the bus and indicates a desire to board”, and “taps the pass” as separate skills.[22] The same manual teaches a specific signal (hold the pass in front of your chest) and a specific cue (wait until the vehicle stops before approaching).[22] A 15-step bus task analysis treats every step as essential; missing one step stops the trip.[26] Boarding is a distinct phase in travel instruction.[23] Pictures at retrieval improve prospective memory for PWID, so the prompt should appear as a photo at the moment the door opens.[24] TransLink built a virtual bus tour for people who are not comfortable boarding, which shows the demand for rehearsal.[25]

For EasyBoardMake boarding its own step. Wait for the bus to stop. Show the open door. Say “Get on bus 2.” Ask “I am on the bus.” The caregiver sees a boarding check after 45 seconds at the stop. The app does not yet prompt the traveller on a timer, and it has no live bus data. Never treat “I see the bus” as the end of the step.

EasyBoard is not starting from zero. Several products solve part of the problem. Patterns to copy

  • Personal photos and recorded voice, not generic icons.
  • One step at a time, with the current and next step visible.
  • Let people rehearse boarding: the door, the card tap, and the seat.
  • Manual advance when GPS is uncertain or steps are close.
  • An explicit, user-initiated route to a human.
  • Trip sharing that the person can see and stop.

Patterns to avoid

  • Turn arrows as the only cue. They are abstract and easy to misread.
  • Automatic step changes based only on GPS. Close steps cause confusion.
  • Camera AR as the only way to navigate. Device, country, and coverage limits exclude people.
  • Always-on sharing as the default. It turns support into surveillance.

One more local finding. Singapore already has schemes that do part of this job: the Helping Hand identifiers (including “Please alert me when I am approaching my stop”), MINDS travel training, the Travel Makers volunteering programme, and Heart Zones. The SBS Transit Travel Buddy is currently focused on wheelchair users; it may extend to other disabilities. EasyBoard should link to these schemes and copy their language, not invent a parallel system.[1][9][8][17]

04 / Principles

Eight rules for the product

These rules turn the research into decisions. When a design debate gets stuck, pick the option that follows the rule. The rules are written for the whole team.

One screen, one decision

Every screen asks for one thing. If a screen has two primary buttons, it has two screens inside it. Split it.

Show the next step, not the whole map

A map is a planning tool. A step is a travelling tool. Show the map when the person asks “where am I?”, not by default.

Calm words, calm colours

Red is for danger. A late bus is not danger. Use plain sentences, one action, and a colour that does not shout.

Help is a normal action

Put “I need help” on the main screen. Do not hide it behind a menu or a long press. Asking for help is part of travelling, not a failure.

The person is the user

The caregiver sets things up and gets alerts. The person with a disability chooses where to go, starts the trip, and decides what to share.

Privacy is visible

Show a clear sharing state in the header while sharing is on. Make pause as easy as start. Explain what is shared in one sentence.

Works when the network doesn’t

Cache the route, the help card, and the caregiver number. Offer SMS and a phone call. A safety feature has to work on a weak connection.

Test with PWID, and pay them

Include people who read little, people who use AAC (communication aids), and people who travel alone. Run the test on a real route.

A useful test for any new screen. Cover the screen and ask the person: “What can you do here?” If they cannot answer in one sentence, the screen is not finished.

05 / Today

What EasyBoard does now

An audit of the implemented app in runtimes/app. The core trip is shipped. The remaining gaps are live bus data, offline support, caregiver alerts, and a full accessibility pass.

What shipped in this build

  • Two safe routes. Saved places and “Another place” open the real Google transit directions. Only caregiver journeys open Guided Trip Mode. The app no longer invents a bus number or stop count.
  • Guided Trip Mode. Story, readiness checks, wait, boarding confirmation, card tap, ride, alight, and arrival. Every journey step, including custom ones, gets a screen. Each step has one primary action and a visible “I’m not sure”.
  • Help Card. Name, destination, current location sentence, caregiver number, call, full-screen “show this to someone”, and a separate 999 link. The number always comes from the saved profile.
  • Privacy choices. Trip only, always, and paused. The header shows the current state. Pause stops location and clears the shared trip record. Settings are stored per user; the privacy choice also syncs to the traveller’s profile.
  • Journeys. A caregiver can add a destination, bus number, and up to eight steps, with photos from the camera or library. Custom steps and edited walk, sit, and arrive steps are used in the live flow. Saves are transactional so two caregivers do not overwrite each other.
  • Caregiver view. One card per person, with stage, actual sharing state, last location, call, and journey setup. Each boarding check calls the person it belongs to. If a traveller stops sharing, the last known location remains visible.
  • Reading support. Three text sizes, an Easy read setting, and read aloud on the trip screens. Preferences persist on native and are stored per user. New controls have labels and larger targets.
  • Home basics. Saved-place photos, “Another place”, pull to refresh, a manual always-share toggle, and a caregiver call button are back.
  • Notifications. Native dev builds and production web register for push. Web uses a Firebase service worker.
  • One codebase. The same screens run on iOS, Android, and web.

What is still missing

  • Live bus data. No arrival time, route change, or vehicle feed. The boarding check uses elapsed time, not a bus arriving.
  • Automatic step advance. There are no geofences. The person or caregiver advances every step.
  • Offline mode and SMS. The help card only works while the app is open. There is no offline cache, SMS fallback, second contact, or keep-awake.
  • Voice notes. Caregivers can add photos, but not record audio for a step.
  • Caregiver alerts. No push alerts for arrival, ETA, off-route, or no-movement. The location page shows a last known point but no stage or ETA.
  • Reduced motion and a full screen-reader pass. The new flows have labels, but the old directions, transit, login, and onboarding screens still need an accessibility audit.
  • Live field evidence. The build has not been tested with PWID on a real route, with VoiceOver or TalkBack, or in airplane mode.

06 / Features

What is shipped and what comes next

Nine features kept from the original proposal. The priority filter still shows what was planned first. The status badge on each card says what is actually in the app now.

P0Shipped

Guided Trip Mode

The old directions screen was a list of text steps. It assumed the person could read, remember where they were in the list, and decide when to move on. That is the exact set of skills the research says is hard.

What it does. Turns a saved route into a sequence of single-purpose cards. Each active card has one instruction, a scene illustration, a read-aloud control, and one primary action. The traveller advances manually. Journey photos appear in the story, not on the active step. Boarding is its own step. The app does not step-advance with GPS or use live bus data.

  1. Before the trip: show the Trip Story and a short readiness check (fare card, phone battery, headphones).
  2. At each step: one sentence, a scene illustration, read aloud, and one primary action.
  3. When the bus arrives: “The bus has stopped” opens the boarding card. “Get on the bus” asks “I am on the bus”. A confirmation moves to the card tap, then the ride.
  4. If the person is unsure: “I’m not sure” opens the Help Card. Call caregiver is always visible.
  5. If a step is missed: “The bus did not stop” keeps the person waiting. “I missed my stop” says “Stay on the bus” and offers a caregiver call.
  6. At arrival: “You are here” clears the trip locally and in Firebase when the person taps Done.

“Wait for the bus. Stand where you can see it.” · “The bus has stopped. The door is open. Check the number your caregiver entered.” · “Tap your card. Wait for the green light.” · “Ride to your stop. Watch for your stop.” · “Get off at your stop. Then walk to your destination.”

Effort
Large; shipped. Local trip model, story, step player, Firebase active-trip sync. No geofence or offline cache yet.
Evidence
Context-aware prompts and step-by-step guidance improved independent travel in small studies.[3] Travel-training assessments treat “identifies the bus and indicates a desire to board” as a separate skill, and photos at retrieval support prospective memory.[22][24]
Risk
Manual advance can be missed. The person may tap “I am on the bus” too early. There is no live bus data. Mitigation: explicit confirmation, a driver script, a caregiver boarding check after 45 seconds, and a call button.
Success signal
More trips completed without a caregiver call. More bus arrivals followed by boarding within 60 seconds. Shorter recovery time after a missed stop.

Easy Read and Read Aloud

Easy read changes how a screen reads. It needs short sentences, one idea per screen, and images that match the words. The NHS guidance asks for about 15 words per sentence and testing with the audience.[6] The CDC guide is stricter for people with very low literacy: 10 words or fewer, one idea per screen, and literal images.[18] The evidence is mixed, so test it with the people who will use it.[21]

What it does. Adds a reading settings panel with Easy read, Normal, Large, and Larger text. The trip screens have a read-aloud control using the phone voice. Reading settings are stored per user on the device. The privacy choice also syncs to the account.

  1. Text: the new trip, help, privacy, journey, and caregiver screens use short, literal sentences.
  2. Layout: Easy read adds spacing and a small type increase; the three text sizes scale the new screens.
  3. Images: caregiver journey photos and scene illustrations. There is no image on/off setting.
  4. Audio: expo-speech reads the trip story and each active step. It is not yet on every screen or button.
  5. Fallback: the text always stays visible. There is no caregiver-recorded name or voice note.
Effort
Medium; core shipped. Settings panel, design tokens, and speech on the trip. No account sync.
Evidence
NHS England easy read guidance; W3C COGA design patterns for clear purpose, familiar controls, and help.[6][5]
Risk
Text-to-speech mispronounces names and places. Mitigation: show the written name too. A caregiver recording feature is still missing.
Success signal
Task success on the top five journeys. Fewer “what does this mean?” questions in testing.
P0Shipped

Help Card

People avoid asking strangers or staff for help because it is embarrassing and hard to explain. A card does the explaining for them. The UK research recommended free assistance cards; the JAM Card shows the model works at scale.[7][10]

What it does. A full-screen card with the person’s name, destination, current location sentence, caregiver name and number, and a plain sentence: “Please call my caregiver.” The person can show it, call, or use the separate 999 link. A driver script sits below: “I am going to Work. Please tell me when to get off.”

  1. Open: one tap from the home screen and from every trip step.
  2. Show: large text, high contrast, and a full-screen mode for showing a stranger.
  3. Call: one button to the caregiver. There is no second contact yet.
  4. Emergency: a separate 999 link. It is never dialled automatically.
  5. Offline: the card says “Works while this app is open.” It is not cached for offline use.
Effort
Small to medium; core shipped. No SMS, photo, second contact, or keep-awake yet.
Evidence
UK DfT assistance-card recommendation; JAM Card adoption; local Helping Hand identifiers.[7][10][1]
Risk
Oversharing personal details to strangers. Mitigation: destination and caregiver number only; no home address; no photo.
Success signal
Help card opened on real trips. Successful handovers to staff or caregivers. People say they would use it again.
P0Shipped

Trip Story

A route line is an abstract drawing. A story is a sequence of places the person can recognise. The MINDS travel training example shows the power of photos, placards, and repetition.[8]

What it does. Before the trip, shows a visual timeline: walk to the stop, wait for the bus, get on, tap the card, sit near the driver, ride to the stop, get off, arrive. It uses the caregiver’s journey steps and photos. The editor starts new journeys with a generic eight-step template; the live trip never falls back to invented route facts.

  1. Create: the caregiver adds up to 8 steps and a photo for any step.
  2. Preview: the person sees the full timeline before starting. There is no swipe-through or per-step voice note.
  3. Practice: “Practice this trip” runs the same steps without sharing or publishing an active trip. It has no quiz or sign-off.
  4. Start: “Start trip” opens the first step and starts sharing if the privacy mode allows it.
Boarding is a step. The story shows the open door, the card tap, and the seat. The live trip asks for confirmation. A generic map pin is not a landmark.
Effort
Medium; core shipped. Photo upload and step editor are live. Offline storage and per-step audio are not.
Evidence
Photo and landmark prompts improved navigation in small studies; travel training improved confidence.[3][2] A meta-analysis of visual activity schedules found a large effect on independent skills (Tau-U 0.96).[19] Virtual bus tours are used to rehearse boarding, and pictures at retrieval support prospective memory.[25][24]
Risk
Old photos confuse the person. Mitigation is still missing: a “photo updated” date and a termly caregiver review.
Success signal
More people start a trip after viewing the story. Fewer wrong-stop incidents. Fewer boarding delays.
P0Partly shipped

Calm change alerts

Uncertainty is the main source of anxiety. A late bus, a platform change, or a missed stop can end the trip. The UK research found that real-time information and clear alternatives reduced stress.[7]

What it does. Handles the changes the app can see without live data. When the bus does not stop, the wait screen offers a calm next step. When the person misses a stop, the ride screen says stay on the bus and offers a call. The caregiver sees a boarding check after 45 seconds at the stop.

  1. Delay: Not shipped. There is no live arrival feed.
  2. Missed stop: Shipped. “Call your caregiver now. Stay on the bus until you can get off safely.”
  3. Off route: Not shipped. No location rule or “Are you okay?” prompt.
  4. No movement: Not shipped. No 10-minute inactivity alert.
  5. Boarding check: Shipped for the caregiver, based on elapsed time at the waiting stage.
Effort
Core partly shipped. Live delay, off-route, and no-movement rules are still needed.
Evidence
UK DfT real-time information findings; W3C COGA error prevention and clear next steps.[7][5]
Risk
False alarms annoy caregivers and frighten the person. Mitigation: confidence thresholds, “I’m okay” button, caregiver confirms before calling. The current boarding check is a timer, not a bus signal.
Success signal
Fewer panic calls. Faster recovery. Caregivers say alerts are useful, not noisy.
P1Partly shipped

Caregiver Co-pilot

A live dot does not tell a caregiver what is happening. Caregivers need to know “is he on the bus?”, “is he stuck?”, “when will he arrive?”. Watching a dot all day does not scale to a classroom or a family with two jobs.[1]

What it does. Replaces the dashboard with one card per person. Each card shows the current stage, last update, and actual sharing state. Call is always available. Location or journey setup sits next to it. The map is one tap away.

  1. Person card: “Ryan · Waiting at the stop · updated 9:12” and a three-step progress bar.
  2. Alerts: a boarding check after 45 seconds at the stop. No push alerts, arrival notification, off-route, or no-movement alert yet.
  3. Location and journey: see the live map while sharing, or set up a journey from the same card.
  4. Cross-device: the active trip is read from Firebase, so a caregiver on another phone sees the same stage.
Effort
Medium; core shipped. Active-trip sync, people cards, and boarding check are live. ETA and push alerts are not.
Evidence
Caregiver burden and the need for exceptions, not continuous monitoring.[1][12]
Risk
Becomes surveillance by another name. Mitigation: trip-only sharing, visible consent, no history by default.
Success signal
Fewer “where are you?” calls. Caregivers respond to alerts within 2 minutes.
P1Partly shipped

Practice Mode

Travel training works through repetition, modelling, and safe mistakes. MINDS used 16 weeks of accompanied trips and simulated problems such as missing a stop. An app can support that routine between sessions.[8]

What it does. “Practice this trip” runs the same story and step flow without publishing an active trip and without sharing location. It is a safe rehearsal, not a lesson.

  1. Rehearse: the story and steps, with photos and read aloud, without live location.
  2. Practice safety: missed bus and missed stop paths can be tried without alerting a caregiver.
  3. No quiz yet: there are no “what do you do next?” questions or picture answers.
  4. No sign-off: the caregiver cannot mark a route as practised, and the home screen does not label it “The way we practised”.
Effort
Medium; rehearsal shipped. Quiz, simulation scoring, and caregiver sign-off are still needed.
Evidence
Travel training improved self-efficacy; video and photo prompts improved route learning.[2][3]
Risk
Practice success is mistaken for real-world readiness. Mitigation: caregiver sign-off and a real accompanied trip. Neither is in the app yet.
Success signal
More first solo trips after practice. Fewer help requests on practised routes.
P1Partly shipped

Voice notes and landmark photos

A caregiver’s voice is familiar and can carry meaning that text cannot. “Turn left at the blue gate” is easier than “continue for 120 metres”. Personal landmarks also solve the last-100-metres problem that maps handle badly.

What it does. Lets the caregiver attach a photo to any journey step from the camera or photo library. The trip story and the active step show the photo. Voice notes are not implemented.

  1. Record: Not shipped. There is no audio recorder or transcript.
  2. Attach: Shipped. Photo, step name, and instructions.
  3. Play: The read-aloud control reads the step text. A caregiver voice cannot be played because it cannot be recorded.
  4. Update: Shipped. The caregiver can change or remove a photo.
Effort
Photos shipped. Audio recording, storage, playback, and transcript remain.
Evidence
Visual and auditory prompts are the most common design elements in the transport-tech review.[3]
Risk
Old photos confuse the person. Audio privacy, unclear speech, and language mismatch wait for the voice-note feature. Mitigation: photo date and termly review, neither shipped yet.
Success signal
Photos present on real steps. Fewer wrong turns near the destination. Voice-note use is not measurable yet.
P1Shipped

Privacy Center

Location sharing is useful only if the person trusts it. Research on surveillance in care finds a tension between safety and autonomy, and PWID describe restriction as harmful.[12][11]

What it does. One screen that answers two questions: what is shared now, and when will it stop? It uses three choices, a plain-language preview, and a sign-out section. The caregiver cannot change it from their screen.

  1. This trip only: share while a trip is active. This is the default.
  2. Always: share until the person turns it off. The header shows the state.
  3. Paused: the app stops sharing, clears the shared trip, and the header shows Paused. The native background task is stopped on the OFF path.
  4. Preview and sign out: the person sees the current state and can sign out of this phone.
Effort
Small to medium; core shipped. No history control, viewer list, or supported-decision record.
Evidence
Surveillance ethics; supported decision-making; visible consent.[12][11][16]
Risk
Too many choices. Mitigation: three presets, one sentence each, caregiver cannot change without the person seeing it.
Success signal
People can explain what is shared. Pause is used without conflict. Fewer people turn sharing off entirely.

Sequencing matters. The trip model and Help Card are shipped, but offline cache is not. Do that before adding live bus data. Easy Read and Read Aloud still need to reach the remaining screens. Privacy Center is small but it protects trust, so keep it in the core release.

Trip flow rules

  • Never show more than one primary action.
  • Keep the help button visible on every step.
  • Let the person advance manually. The shipped app has no geofence.
  • Boarding is a step. Do not assume seeing the bus means getting on.
  • Cache the route before the trip starts. This is still next.

Help flow rules

  • Open the card in one tap.
  • Keep the caregiver number readable at arm’s length.
  • Never auto-dial emergency services.
  • Do not promise offline use until it is cached.
  • Second contact and SMS are still next.

Consent flow rules

  • Trip-only sharing is the default.
  • Show the current state and stage to the person.
  • Pause is a normal action, not an exception.
  • No silent changes by the caregiver.
  • History controls and a caregiver-view preview are still next.

How to make the next part real

This is the practical layer: what is already in the build, what still needs work, and the acceptance criteria that stop accessibility from becoming a one-off cleanup.

What is in the shipped build

Two added packages: expo-speech and @react-native-async-storage/async-storage. The rest uses the existing Expo and Firebase stack. Read aloud goes through useReadAloud. 10 / Test

How to prove it helps

Do not test whether people “like” the app. Test whether they can finish a trip, recover from a problem, and ask for help. Run the test on real routes with real phones.

What this build has been checked for. TypeScript, 320 px and 375 px layouts, the PWID and caregiver views, the real-directions route, saving a journey with no photos, custom steps in the live flow, trip resume after reload, paused sharing clearing the caregiver view, ending a trip, logout clearing local data, and one location timeout producing one error rather than a retry loop. It has not been tested with PWID on a real route, with VoiceOver or TalkBack, in airplane mode, or through production web push. Those are the next tests, not done work.

Who to include

  • 6 to 8 PWID. Mix reading levels, phone confidence, and support needs. Include at least two people who do not read.
  • 3 caregivers. Include one older caregiver and one support worker who manages several people.
  • 2 transport staff or volunteers. They are the people a help card is shown to.
  • 1 speech and language therapist or AAC (communication aid) specialist. Review the copy and the audio.

How to run it

  • Co-design first. Use paper cards and a phone with a clickable prototype. Ask people to show you how they would use it, not whether they like the colour.
  • Task test second. Two real routes: a direct trip and one with a change. Include a bus that stops but the person does not move, a bus that is not theirs, a bus that does not stop, and a missed stop.
  • Help test third. Role-play a stranger or staff member. Can the person show the card and get the right help without speaking?
  • Caregiver test last. Give them the trip card and three alerts. Measure how fast they understand what to do.

What to measure

Trip completionPercentage of trips finished without a caregiver call. Target: +20% over the current app in the same route test.
BoardingPercentage of bus arrivals where the person boards within 60 seconds without a caregiver prompt. Target: 8 of 10.
Right busPercentage of boardings on the correct route, after checking the number. Target: 9 of 10.
Recovery timeTime from a missed stop or delay to a safe next action. Target: under 2 minutes with the app.
Help successPercentage of help-card uses where a stranger or staff member understands what to do. Target: 8 of 10.
ConfidenceThree-face scale before and after the trip. Target: at least one point higher for first-time users.
Caregiver trustCaregivers can say where the person is and what happens next without opening the map. Target: 9 of 10.
Privacy understandingPeople can explain what is shared and with whom. Target: 8 of 10 can answer in one sentence.

  5. When the person cannot consent, who decides, and how is that recorded?

Definition of done. A person who has never used EasyBoard can board the right bus, travel a practised route, recover from a missed bus or missed stop, and ask for help, without reading more than one short sentence at a time. If that is true, the product works.
